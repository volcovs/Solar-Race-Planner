# Bridgestone World Solar Challenge (BWSC): fact sheet

Public facts for the pitch deck and team briefing. Every claim cites a numbered reference (see §8). All sources accessed 2026-11-06.
Team names are left out on purpose. Results give the country only.
**unverified** = we could not confirm the claim against a source we actually read.

---

## 1. History & positioning

- **Origin.** In 1982 Hans Tholstrup and Larry Perkins drove the home-built solar car *Quiet Achiever* across Australia from west to east. That trip inspired the World Solar Challenge [1]. Wikipedia gives the trip as 4,052 km, Sydney to Perth, in 20 days, sponsored by BP, and calls it the event's precursor [3]. Note the conflict: the official site says west to east, Wikipedia says Sydney to Perth (east to west).
- **Founding.** "Inaugurated in 1987 with pioneer sponsor, the South Australian Tourism Commission" [1]. After the 4th event, Tholstrup sold the rights to the State of South Australia [3].
- **Purpose.** The official aim is "to stimulate research into, and development of, energy efficient vehicle designs and technologies to support the global challenge to decarbonise mobility". The regulations add that it is "primarily a design competition" [5].
- **Naming and sponsors.**
  - Called the *Bridgestone* World Solar Challenge since 2013 [3].
  - The Challenger class is now the "Toyota Challenger Class" [2][4].
- **Cadence.** "First held in 1987, the event runs every two years and will celebrate 40-year anniversary in 2027" [2].
  - Editions held: 1987, 1990, 1993, 1996, 1999, then every two years 2001–2019, then 2023 and 2025 [3][1].
  - **2021 was cancelled** because of COVID-19 border closures (announced 12 Feb 2021) [3]. No 2020 event was ever planned.
  - The 2027 regulations call 2027 the "19th edition" [5]. Counting the editions listed above gives 18 held events by 2027, so the regulations' numbering is **unverified**.
- **Field size.**
  - 2019: a record 53 entries from 24 countries, about 1,500 participants [2].
  - 2023: 43 teams from 23 "locations" announced (32 Challenger, 11 Cruiser) [6]. 31 teams started [8][3].
  - 2025: 34 teams started (26 Challenger, 8 Cruiser). 17 finished the full course (14 Challenger, 3 Cruiser) [7]. The official results list 13 countries/economies among Challenger entrants [9] (counted from the results PDF).
- **How the official site describes the event:**
  - Regulations page: "the world's greatest innovation and engineering challenge driving decarbonisation through sustainable mobility" [4].
  - Overview page: "the world's leading innovation challenge" [2].
- **The "oldest solar car competition" claim cannot be defended as stated.**
  - The **Tour de Sol** in Switzerland started in **1985** (Romanshorn to Geneva). Wikipedia calls it "the first rally for solar powered vehicles" [10]. That is two years before BWSC.
  - None of the official BWSC pages we read claim "oldest" or "first" [1][2][4][7].
  - What we can defend: first held 1987; ~40 years old; runs every two years; ~3,000 km across a continent on public highways.
- **Recommended pitch-safe phrasing:**
  > "Since 1987, the Bridgestone World Solar Challenge has sent solar cars 3,000 km across Australia, from Darwin to Adelaide. It is one of the world's longest-running solar car races and is billed by its organisers as 'the world's greatest innovation and engineering challenge'."
  - Do not say "oldest" or "first".
  - "Premier transcontinental solar race" is acceptable as marketing language, but it is our opinion, not a sourced fact.

## 2. Route

- **Start and finish.** Darwin (Northern Territory) to Adelaide (South Australia). The route follows the **Stuart Highway** to Port Augusta, then **Highway 1** into the City of Adelaide [11]. The organisers say "over 3,000 kilometres" [11] and "approximately 3000 km" [5].
- **Distances in the 2025 route notes:**
  - Final control stop at Victoria Park, Adelaide: 3,026.9 km [12].
  - Adelaide urban area begins at about 3,003 km (Waterloo Corner Rd) [12].
  - Wikipedia gives the timed portion as ending at 2,998 km [3].
  - Results report 3,021–3,022 km [3][7].
- **Finish rule.** The race finish time is taken at a marshalling point a few km before the finish line. Cars are then escorted to the line in the city [5].
- **Season.**
  - Earlier editions ran in October (2023: "this October" [6]).
  - **2025 moved to late August, the Australian winter** [3]. The race started in Darwin on Sun 24 Aug 2025 [12]. The organisers described it as a "new winter timeslot with less sun and strong winds" [7].
- **Wind.**
  - The regulations warn: "Wind gusts exceeding 100 km/h are possible", and note that cars have left the road in past events. Crosswind stability analysis is mandatory [5].
  - The route notes flag "high cross winds" near the Ghan railway overpass (~1,140 km) [12].
  - The 2023 race had "unseasonal headwinds" [13].
- **Road conditions.**
  - Open-road speed limits in the Northern Territory reach **130 km/h** [12].
  - The 42 km straight at ~1,126 km is the longest on the route [12].
  - Road trains use truck bays that are part of a fatigue-management programme; teams are advised not to camp in them [12].
  - Many cattle grids and wandering stock [12].
  - Fruit-fly exclusion zone south of Tennant Creek; quarantine checks at the South Australian border [12].
  - Woomera Prohibited Area: roadside camping is allowed but teams must not cross fences [12].
- **Connectivity.**
  - Mobile coverage is patchy. The route notes list individual "mobile hotspots" and places with "No Telstra" [12].
  - Each team is issued a satellite phone and UHF CB radios [5].
- **Climate.** We did not read a source for typical temperatures, dry-season climate or prevailing south-east trade winds. Treat those details as **unverified**.

## 4. Rules relevant to strategy

Source: 2027 Event Regulations V1.0, published 7 May 2026 [5]. The regulations list these changes from 2025: a new Historic class and a new Innovation class, an insurance requirement for certifying engineers, updated occupant-cell rules, a static-stability requirement (SSF > 1, 45° tilt test), and clearer control-stop procedures. **No energy or array changes are listed** [5]. The 2025 values below are from [2][3].

- **Driving hours.**
  - Driving runs 08:00 to 17:00 every day [5].
  - Finishing up to 10 minutes after 17:00 costs 1 minute of penalty per minute. Beyond that, each minute costs 2 minutes. The penalty is applied by delaying the next morning's start time [5].
  - No driving after sunset. Starting early costs 2 minutes per minute [5].
- **Overnight stops.**
  - Teams camp wherever they are at the end of the day; the team chooses the spot and pays for it [5][2].
  - All vehicles and activity must be at least 10 m from the road [5].
  - The Observer marks the stopping point on the road, and the car restarts from that mark [5].
- **Static solar charging windows (Challenger).**
  - The battery pack must be **disconnected and sealed before 19:15** and **not unsealed before 06:15** [5].
  - In practice, evening charging runs from the stop until 19:15 or sunset, and morning charging from 06:15 or sunrise until 08:00. This is our reading of the rules.
  - No mirrors or concentrators; no water sprayed on the array during the day [5].
- **Energy storage limits.**
  - **11 MJ (≈3.06 kWh) for Challenger, 55 MJ (≈15.3 kWh) for Cruiser** [5][2]. The kWh figures are our conversion.
  - The overview describes the basis: "a 1000W car would complete the journey in 50 hours" [2].
  - Energy from motion, regenerative braking, road gradients and wind is accepted [5].
- **Solar array.**
  - Challenger: **at most 6 m²** of solar collector area, counting the rear faces of bifacial cells [5].
  - 2025 raised the limit from 4 m² to 6 m² [3].
- **Classes.**
  - **Toyota Challenger:** single-seat cars, the original concept, optimised for converting sunlight into speed [4][5].
  - **Cruiser:** two or more seats; judged on practicality as well as efficiency [4][5].
  - **Innovation:** non-competitive [4].
  - **Historic:** non-competitive, added for the 40th anniversary in 2027 [4].
  - We found **no "Explorer" class** in official sources. "Explorer" may be a mix-up with another event; **unverified**.
- **Scoring.**
  - **Challenger and Cruiser:** the earliest finish time at the Adelaide marshalling point wins, plus any unserved penalties [5].
  - Teams that do not finish are ranked by how many control stops they completed, then by arrival time at the last one [5].
  - **Cruiser design score (2027):**
    - Judges score each car 0–100% on innovation, access, comfort, ease of operation and charging, versatility, style, suitability for its declared purpose, environmental impact, and the charging system [5].
    - The score becomes a **time advantage**. After Katherine, each Cruiser is held for d = 300·(s* − s)/s* minutes, where s is its score and s* is the best score [5].
  - **Earlier Cruiser formats:**
    - 2013: weighted score (time 56.6%, passengers 5.7%, grid energy 18.9%, practicality 18.9%) [3].
    - 2017: "regularity trial" scored on energy efficiency and practicality [3].
    - 2023: Cruisers had to cover more than 1,000 km between external charges [6].
  - The exact **2025** Cruiser scoring is **unverified**. The official results rank Cruisers by event time [9].
- **External energy (Cruiser only).**
  - Cruisers may recharge from an external source **overnight only**: after finishing driving for the day and before starting the next day [5].
  - Once a Cruiser has started driving for the day, it cannot recharge until it stops for the day [5].
  - Challenger packs must be impossible to charge between sealing and unsealing; the Observer checks this [5].
- **Speed.**
  - Teams must obey all posted civil limits; speeding is penalised [5][12].
  - Driving below 50 km/h in 100+ km/h zones without traffic is penalised [5].
  - A team that cannot hold **60 km/h** on the open road is "no longer competing" [5].
- **Support convoy.**
  - Mandatory front and rear escort vehicles with amber flashing lights [5].
  - The front escort must stay within 500 m of the car; the rear escort within 5 s [5].
  - An Observer rides in the rear escort [5].
  - The car carries an organiser-supplied tracker and data logger [5].
- **Drivers.**
  - 3–6 registered drivers [5].
  - Each driver is ballasted to at least 80 kg [5].
- **Exclusion.** Teams can be excluded for 3 or more demerit points, or for driving without a rear escort [5].

## 7. Why it's a great data and strategy problem

- **Fixed energy, variable sun.** Each car carries only 11 MJ of storage; everything else must come from the sun, regenerative braking or wind [5][2]. The winter timeslot has less sun and strong winds [7].
- **The weather moves the result.** 2023 had unseasonal headwinds [13]. 2025 had wind gusts, driving rain and a weather-delayed start [14]. The regulations warn of gusts above 100 km/h [5].
- **Fine margins.** In 2025 the top three were separated by about 30 minutes after roughly 35 hours of racing [7][9]. Nine Challengers finished on day five within 4 h 7 min of each other [7].
- **Hard constraints shape the plan.** Strategy has to fit:
  - the 08:00–17:00 driving window and its escalating overtime penalties [5];
  - nine 30-minute control stops with closing deadlines [12][5];
  - pack sealing from 19:15 to 06:15 [5];
  - a 60 km/h minimum to stay competitive [5].
- **Energy mistakes end races.** In 2023 half the Cruiser field drove close to 1,000 km before charging, then could not finish the next stage [13].
